Reading blood oxygen with light

This is a working pulse oximeter, the kind used at your bedside in hospital. It measures how much oxygen is in your blood using nothing but two coloured lights and a sensor. This demonstration shows you how the reading is built, and where it can go wrong.

No prior knowledge required. Takes about two minutes.

Before you start

How the sensor works

Four steps, from light to number.

1

Two beams of light shine through your finger

A red LED and an infrared LED sit on one side of your fingertip, pulsing on and off many times a second.

2

Blood absorbs each colour differently

Blood carrying oxygen absorbs more infrared light and lets more red light through. Blood without oxygen does the opposite. This difference is the entire basis of the measurement.

3

A detector measures what survives the trip

A photodetector on the other side of your finger reads how much of each colour made it through, once per heartbeat.

4

An algorithm turns the ratio into a percentage

The ratio of red to infrared light is converted into the SpO2 number you would see on a hospital monitor. This calculation assumes the light passes through skin the same way for everyone — it does not.

What this simulator shows

Pulse oximeters were developed and tested mostly on light skin. Multiple clinical studies have found they can overestimate blood oxygen in patients with darker skin, sometimes missing a dangerously low reading entirely. This tool lets you recreate that effect.

1

Set the true oxygen level

Lower it to simulate a patient whose blood oxygen is genuinely dropping.

2

Change skin tone and perfusion

Both affect how much light reaches the detector in the first place.

3

Compare the device reading to the truth

Then try the correction toggle, and see how much of the gap it can actually close.

Glossary & method

The terms and calculations behind the numbers on screen.

SpO2
The oxygen saturation estimated by the sensor and shown on the display. This is what a clinician reads.
SaO2
The actual oxygen saturation in the blood, measured directly. In this demonstration, the simulator lets you set this value yourself.
Perfusion index (PI)
A measure of pulsing blood flow at the sensor site. Cold hands, low blood pressure and poor circulation all reduce it, weakening the optical signal.
Monk Skin Tone scale (MST)
A 10-point scale, from 1 (lightest) to 10 (darkest), developed to test how well technology performs across the full range of human skin tones.
Ratio of ratios
The core calculation: the sensor compares how much red and infrared light was absorbed, normalised against the steady background absorption of skin and tissue, to estimate oxygenation.
Occult hypoxemia
A dangerously low true oxygen level that the device fails to flag because its displayed reading stays in the normal range.
